The most expensive mistake we see in Chandler is treating deep excavation design as a standard shoring plan. A contractor opens a cut for a parking garage off the Loop 202, hits a dense caliche layer at 12 feet, and the vertical walls stand beautifully—for two days. Then the caliche softens at the interface with the underlying sandy gravel, a chunk spalls, and the adjacent utility trench collapses. The repair bill triples the original excavation budget. Chandler’s subsurface is not uniform; it transitions from coarse alluvial fan deposits near the San Tan Mountains to finer basin fill toward the west, with cemented caliche horizons that can be either a blessing or a trap depending on groundwater and loading. Our team approaches each excavation with a site-specific earth pressure model that accounts for these transitions. When the upper soils are stiff but fractured, we often calibrate parameters using field data from CPT testing to avoid the sample disturbance that makes caliche strengths look deceptively high in the lab.

Caliche in Chandler can stand vertical for days—until water softens the interface, and then it fails without warning. We design for that interface.

How we work

The soil contrast between West Chandler and the Price Road Corridor illustrates why a single design parameter set never works here. West Chandler, near the old agricultural parcels south of Ray Road, typically has fine-grained basin deposits with higher plasticity—so lateral earth pressures build gradually, and drainage control dominates the temporary support design. The Price Road employment corridor sits on coarser alluvial fans from the Santan Mountains, where caliche lenses up to three feet thick can bridge vertically but fail suddenly if perched water accumulates behind the cut. Our design methodology for each zone starts with differentiating drained versus undrained behavior based on excavation rate and proximity to irrigation canals. We run staged finite-element models in PLAXIS where the excavation sequence, dewatering schedule, and bracing stiffness are time-stepped, and we validate those models against inclinometer data from previous Chandler projects. The output is a construction-sequence drawing set that tells the contractor exactly when each strut or tieback must be installed, not just where.

Local geotechnical context

Chandler’s transformation from a small agricultural town in the 1920s into a high-tech manufacturing hub means we are frequently excavating next to structures built decades apart under different codes. A deep cut for a new data center might abut a 1970s concrete masonry building on spread footings, with no as-built records. In these cases, the risk is not just global stability—it is differential settlement that cracks the neighbor’s slab before the shoring deflects enough to trigger a threshold alarm. We have seen pre-construction surveys miss hairline cracks that become legal disputes once excavation starts. Our design approach for these tight sites adds a settlement analysis under lateral unloading, not just basal heave. We specify pre-excavation grouting of loose zones when the grouting program can be tailored to fill voids at the caliche-gravel interface, and we set movement triggers at 50% of the code-allowed maximum so the contractor has time to react before damage occurs.

How do you account for caliche layers in the shoring design?

We sample caliche carefully—triple-tube core barrels or pitcher samplers when the cementation is strong—and run unconfined compression tests to quantify strength. Then we model it as a brittle material in PLAXIS with post-peak softening, so the design accounts for the drop in strength after cracking. The key is not to rely on caliche’s peak strength for long-term load cases.

What is the typical submittal timeline for a deep excavation permit in Chandler?

After we receive the approved geotechnical report and architectural foundation plans, the excavation design and drawings take three to four weeks for a typical 25-foot cut. City of Chandler plan review for shoring typically adds two to three weeks, provided the submittal includes the required calculations sealed by an Arizona-registered engineer.
