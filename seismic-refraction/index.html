The most expensive mistake we see in Chandler isn't a cracked slab. It's a contractor who sinks a drill rig into a buried paleochannel nobody mapped. The Salt River and its ancient tributaries left a mess of hidden channels and cemented gravels under the southeast valley. You hit a void or a soft lens at 30 feet, and suddenly your pad design is toast. We run seismic refraction and reflection surveys to give you a continuous profile before you commit to a borehole grid. The velocity data tells you where the caliche is solid and where it's not. For deeper targets or when you need shear-wave velocity for site class, we pair the tomography with a MASW survey. The combination locks in your Vs30 without the guesswork that comes from sparse SPT data alone.

A single seismic line can flag a buried channel that three evenly spaced borings would miss entirely.

Methodology and scope

Chandler sits on a basin floor where the summer heat bakes the surface clay into brick but the monsoon storms saturate the upper ten feet in hours. That contrast plays tricks on seismic signals. Our crew has learned to shoot lines at dawn during July and August, when the surface is still dry and the ambient noise from construction sites hasn't kicked in. We use a 24-channel seismograph with geophone spacing down to five feet for high-resolution refraction. The P-wave velocity cross-section we deliver separates the stiff caliche, the intermediate alluvium, and the underlying basin fill. On reflection surveys we chase the bedrock interface and map fault strands that ASCE 7 demands you consider for seismic site class. Every profile comes with a layered velocity model and a plain-language summary written by an engineer who has actually graded dirt in Maricopa County.

Local considerations

Chandler grew fast. In the 1980s, a lot of farmland south of Ray Road turned into subdivisions and tilt-up warehouses in under a decade. The geotechnical reports from that era often relied on a handful of drive samples per parcel. We've re-scanned sites near the Price Corridor and found buried stream channels twenty feet wide that predate the Holocene. If your structural engineer is using a generic site class D assumption based on old SPT data, you might be overdesigning the lateral system or, worse, underestimating the seismic demand. IBC Chapter 16 ties your design ground motion directly to the shear-wave velocity of the upper 100 feet. A seismic tomography survey gives you a measured Vs profile instead of a default value. For projects near the Loosely-Cemented Transition Zone on the basin margin, that measurement can drop your site class from D to C and save real money on the frame.

Associated technical services

01

Seismic Refraction Tomography

A 2D P-wave velocity profile that maps the top of caliche, depth to competent bearing strata, and lateral changes in alluvial stiffness. We shoot overlapping spreads and process the data with a tomographic inversion algorithm that handles the strong velocity inversions common in desert soils. You get a color-contoured cross-section, plus a table of interpreted engineering layers with their seismic velocities.

02

Seismic Reflection Profiling

High-resolution SH-wave reflection for imaging the bedrock surface and basin faults. We use a vibratory source to generate a clean shear-wave signal and stack the data to suppress the cultural noise from Loop 202 traffic and nearby compressor stations. The final section is depth-converted and tied to any available boring logs.

Can you shoot seismic lines on asphalt or concrete pavement?

Yes, and we do it regularly in Chandler's industrial parks. We bolt the geophones to the pavement with a quick-set epoxy or use sandbags for coupling. The source energy couples well with asphalt, and the data quality is usually excellent. We flag any shallow utilities from a One-Call locate before we start.

How deep can you see with reflection versus refraction?

Refraction gives us a reliable velocity model down to about one-fifth of the spread length. With a 240-foot spread, that means 40 to 50 feet of solid coverage in typical basin alluvium. Reflection goes deeper—we've imaged the bedrock at 200 feet in the Chandler Heights area using an SH-wave source and a longer receiver array. The right tool depends on your target depth.
