The first step in any retaining wall design in Chandler is understanding what lies beneath the surface. Our approach starts with a truck-mounted CME-75 drill rig moving into position on the site, capable of advancing hollow-stem augers through the cemented caliche layers that define the subsurface here. In Maricopa County, the transition from the upper fine-grained alluvium to the deeper, harder conglomerate at approximately 12 to 17 feet dictates the excavation depth and the type of shoring required. Our team logs the cuttings in real time, comparing them against the regional stratigraphy developed by the Arizona Geological Survey, and extracts undisturbed Shelby tube samples right at the depth where the wall stem will experience maximum bending moment. Before finalizing the stem geometry, we often correlate these field logs with an in-situ permeability test to quantify the drainage demands behind the wall, because standing water in Chandler’s semi-arid summer monsoon bursts is the single most common cause of block wall distress.

In Chandler’s caliche terrain, the failure plane rarely follows the textbook—it tracks the interface between cemented hardpan and loose alluvium, a detail missed by generic designs.

How we work

Chandler sits at an elevation of 1,214 feet, on a gentle slope that drains toward the Gila River basin. This subtle topography masks a complex soil profile where the surface sandy loam gives way to a hard caliche-rich horizon. For a typical 7-foot cantilever wall, the design must balance the active earth pressure coefficient—calculated using the Rankine theory for the cohesionless upper layers and modified by the Arizona-specific soil parameters from our grain-size analysis results—against the passive resistance in front of the buried footing. Our analyses incorporate the phi angle back-calculated from direct shear tests, the unit weight of the native soil at 120 pcf, and a surcharge load of 250 psf for adjacent residential driveways. The stem thickness, determined through ACI 318-19 moment capacity checks, typically ranges from 8 to 12 inches for walls under 10 feet. We specify a continuous drainage composite or a gravel-filled zone with a 4-inch perforated outlet every 50 feet, because the short-duration, high-intensity rainfall events in the Sonoran Desert can generate hydrostatic pressures that exceed the design assumptions by a factor of two if not properly relieved. For taller structures exceeding 12 feet, we integrate a global analysis that evaluates the deep-seated failure surface passing beneath the footing.

Local geotechnical context

The mistake that trips up local contractors almost every time is treating the caliche as a uniform solid rock. In reality, the upper vadose zone in Chandler weathers the carbonate cementation into a brittle crust, while a few feet lower the deposit remains a weakly cemented sand. An excavator digs through the crust easily, the foreman assumes he has a stiff bearing stratum, and the wall is founded on a material that loses half its strength when saturated by a leaking irrigation line. Two monsoons later, the footing has rotated outward 4 degrees and the block joints have opened up. Our design process eliminates this risk by mapping the exact elevation of the competent bearing layer through a combination of dynamic cone penetration and laboratory slake durability tests on the caliche cores. We then specify a key depth that extends at least 18 inches into the unweathered portion of the hardpan, a detail that transforms the failure probability from a gamble into an engineered certainty. For walls near retention basins, we also run a rapid drawdown scenario because the pore pressure equalization lag in the silty backfill can temporarily reverse the safety factor.

Other technical services

01

Geotechnical Investigation for Wall Design

Subsurface exploration using hollow-stem auger borings advanced to a depth of twice the wall height. The program includes SPT testing per ASTM D1586 at 5-foot intervals, laboratory classification of the caliche-affected layers, and direct shear tests on reconstituted samples compacted to 95% of the standard Proctor maximum. We deliver a bearing capacity recommendation and a lateral earth pressure diagram specific to the site.

02

Structural Analysis and Reinforcement Detailing

Calculation of stem moments, heel and toe shear, and total overturning stability using the Equivalent Fluid Pressure method calibrated to the site-specific Ka. We produce rebar schedules and section details compliant with ACI 318-19, including the development length checks for epoxy-coated bars in the sulfate-rich soil environment found in parts of eastern Chandler.

03

Construction Phase Observation and Instrumentation

Site visits during key stages: footing subgrade inspection, backfill placement and compaction testing, and drainage system installation. For walls exceeding 15 feet of exposed height, we can install inclinometer casing behind the stem to monitor the rotational movement during the first year of service, verifying that the actual performance aligns with the predicted deflection of less than 0.5% of the height.

Questions and answers

How much does a retaining wall design for a residential property in Chandler typically cost?

For a standard single-family residential lot with a wall height under 8 feet, the engineering design fee ranges from US$1,170 to US$4,460. The exact figure depends on the length of the wall, the complexity of the soil profile—particularly the depth to competent caliche—and whether the municipality requires a full drainage report. This fee covers the geotechnical boring, the structural calculations, and the signed, wet-stamped construction drawings.

What is the difference between a cantilever wall and a segmental block wall in terms of structural behavior?

A reinforced concrete cantilever wall resists lateral earth pressure through the moment capacity of its vertical stem and the weight of the soil on the heel slab; it acts as a monolithic unit. A segmental block wall relies on its own mass and the frictional interaction between the block units and the geogrid reinforcement layers to form a coherent gravity mass. In Chandler, the choice often comes down to the stiffness of the foundation material: a cantilever wall tolerates minor differential settlement better, while a segmental wall requires a more uniform bearing stratum to prevent the facing from stepping out of alignment.

How do you handle the drainage behind the wall in a climate with intense monsoon storms?

The drainage design for a Chandler retaining wall must accommodate a short-duration, high-intensity inflow. We specify a 12-inch-wide zone of open-graded ASTM #57 stone wrapped in a non-woven geotextile filter fabric, with a 4-inch diameter perforated collector pipe at the footing level. The pipe is sloped at a minimum of 1% to a solid outlet that discharges to the street or a swale. We also detail a surface swale or a curb at the top of the wall to prevent runoff from cascading over the face, which would otherwise erode the backfill and expose the upper course of block or the concrete stem.

Do you use the Rankine or Coulomb method for calculating lateral earth pressure, and why?

We use the Coulomb method for most Chandler projects because it accounts for the wall-soil interface friction angle, which reduces the active thrust relative to the simpler Rankine solution. This reduction is significant for the rough, cast-in-place concrete surface of a cantilever stem. For segmental walls, we adopt the coherent gravity method as outlined in the NCMA design manual, which extends the Coulomb wedge theory to the reinforced soil mass. In both cases, we validate the assumed failure surface geometry against the actual layering of the caliche and alluvial deposits encountered in the boring logs.
