Chandler sits at roughly 1,210 feet in the East Valley of the Phoenix metro area, and while Arizona is not typically the first state that comes to mind for seismic risk, the tectonic reality is more nuanced than most people assume. The city rests within the Basin and Range province, where extensional forces have created a network of Quaternary faults—some of which, like the Lone Mountain fault, lie less than 30 miles from downtown. A seismic microzonation study here does not look for the same shaking you would expect in California, but rather maps how the deep alluvial sediments of the Salt River basin can amplify modest bedrock motions into something structurally significant. When the Maricopa Association of Governments updated its regional hazard plan, the conversation around basin-edge effects in Chandler became far more concrete. Our work in the area has repeatedly shown that site class D and E soils, which are common near the Price Corridor, demand a level of attention that generic county hazard maps simply cannot provide. For projects where liquefaction potential needs to be ruled out before foundation design, we often pair microzonation with a targeted liquefaction assessment based on SPT blow counts and fines content.

Basin-edge amplification in the East Valley can turn a 0.08g bedrock motion into a 0.18g surface demand—more than doubling the short-period spectral acceleration without changing the seismic source.

Methodology and scope

A mistake we see too often in East Valley engineering is treating the entire Phoenix metro as a single uniform site class, relying on outdated USGS Quaternary geology maps that do not capture the variability between Chandler’s older floodplain deposits and the younger channel fill along the Santan Vista. A proper microzonation campaign corrects this by executing a grid of geophysical measurements—typically MASW or downhole seismic—to constrain the time-averaged shear-wave velocity in the upper 30 meters at multiple points across the parcel. The resulting Vs30 map becomes the foundation for assigning NEHRP site classes with spatial precision, and when combined with borehole logs, it reveals where impedance contrasts between stiff caliche layers and loose silty sand might generate localized amplification peaks. In Chandler, the water table often sits between 80 and 150 feet deep, which means most shallow profiles are dry and the velocity contrast at the capillary fringe is less pronounced than in coastal basins, but the presence of cemented paleosols can still produce sharp impedance jumps that standard 100-foot borings miss. We sometimes integrate MASW surveys to extend the shear-wave profile beyond the depth of conventional drilling, especially when a project requires a site-specific ground motion analysis per ASCE 7 Chapter 21.

Local considerations

A three-story medical office building near the Loop 202 Santan Freeway was designed in 2019 using the default Site Class D assumption from the ASCE 7 hazard tool, but the geotechnical investigation had only gone to 40 feet and missed a thick sequence of very loose sandy silt between 45 and 65 feet. When our team ran a follow-up MASW line and a seismic cone penetration test, the measured Vs30 dropped to 195 m/s, pushing the site firmly into Site Class E territory and increasing the design spectral response at one-second period by nearly 40 percent. The structural engineer had to revisit the lateral force-resisting system and add moment frames on two grid lines, which caused a six-week delay and a significant change order. In Chandler, where commercial development along the Price Corridor and the Chandler Airpark area continues at a rapid pace, skipping a detailed microzonation study because the USGS national map shows ‘low’ hazard is a gamble that can quietly undermine the seismic performance objectives of an otherwise well-designed building. The team also recommended a complementary seismic refraction survey to better delineate the top of the cemented layer before finalizing the foundation recommendations.

Associated technical services

01

Site-Specific Ground Motion Hazard Analysis

We run probabilistic and deterministic seismic hazard calculations using the USGS Unified Hazard Tool and site-specific amplification factors derived from measured Vs profiles, providing design spectra that replace the default ASCE 7 maps.

02

2D Basin Response Modeling

For large footprints near the basin margin, one-dimensional SHAKE analyses often underestimate long-period amplification; we use finite-difference or spectral-element codes to capture lateral wave propagation effects across the sediment-rock interface.

03

Liquefaction Potential Index (LPI) Mapping

Integrated with the microzonation grid, we calculate LPI and lateral spreading displacement using SPT data corrected for fines content and overburden, producing parcel-scale hazard maps for foundation design.

04

Probabilistic Seismic Hazard Deaggregation

We identify the dominant magnitude-distance pairs controlling the hazard at the site, which helps structural engineers select and scale appropriate ground motion records for nonlinear time-history analysis.

Frequently asked questions

Does Chandler really need a seismic microzonation study if it is not in a high-seismicity zone like California?

The question comes up often, and the short answer is that building code compliance does not depend on being near the San Andreas. Chandler falls under the IBC and ASCE 7, which require a site-specific ground motion analysis when Site Class E or F soils are present, or when the structure is assigned to Risk Category III or IV. The deep Salt River Valley sediments that underlie most of Chandler can amplify long-period energy from distant earthquakes in southern California, northern Mexico, or even the Intermountain Seismic Belt, so the design spectrum can be controlled by events 200 miles away. A microzonation study quantifies that amplification instead of assuming it away.

What is the difference between a USGS hazard map and a microzonation study for a specific site in Chandler?

The USGS National Seismic Hazard Model provides uniform-hazard spectra for a reference rock condition (Vs30 = 760 m/s) on a roughly 2 km grid. A microzonation study replaces that generic rock assumption with a measured soil profile at the exact coordinates of your project, applies site-specific amplification factors, and accounts for basin geometry that the national model smooths out. In practical terms, the USGS map might suggest a short-period spectral acceleration of 0.25g at your Chandler site, while a microzonation analysis that measures Vs30 = 220 m/s and a basin depth of 1.8 km could return 0.38g—a 50% difference that directly changes the required lateral system.

Which geophysical methods are most appropriate for a Chandler microzonation campaign?

In the East Valley, the combination of MASW (Multichannel Analysis of Surface Waves) and ReMi (Refraction Microtremor) has proven to be the most cost-effective approach for measuring Vs30 across large parcels, because both methods use ambient noise and a linear array of geophones that can be deployed quickly even in developed areas. When the project requires a high-resolution velocity profile below 30 meters—for example, to identify the depth to the basin floor—we supplement with downhole seismic testing in a cased borehole. Seismic refraction works well for mapping the top of the caliche caprock but loses resolution where velocity inversions exist, which is common in Chandler’s interbedded alluvial sequences.

What is the typical cost range for a seismic microzonation study on a commercial lot in Chandler?

For a commercial parcel between 2 and 10 acres in Chandler, a complete microzonation package—including a MASW grid, one or two calibration boreholes with downhole seismic, site response analysis, and a signed report stamped by a registered geotechnical engineer—typically runs between US$4,480 and US$16,280. The range depends on the number of measurement points required to achieve the spatial resolution the structural engineer needs, the depth of the velocity profile, and whether basin-response modeling or liquefaction mapping is included in the scope. A smaller infill lot with just a single MASW line and a desk-based amplification study will fall toward the lower end of that range.
