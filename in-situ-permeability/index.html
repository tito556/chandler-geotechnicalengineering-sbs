Chandler’s transformation from an agricultural outpost to a tech-manufacturing powerhouse has fundamentally altered local groundwater and surface drainage patterns. What were once flood-irrigated fields are now dense subdivisions and high-tech campuses with zero-lot-line stormwater constraints. The city sits atop Quaternary alluvium with intermittent caliche lenses that can fool a contractor into thinking they have free-draining material when they actually have a perched water table problem. We see it on nearly every mid-rise project west of the Loop 101. Getting accurate in-situ permeability values with a Lefranc test is not just a box to check for the City’s drainage report. It defines whether your retention basin will drain in 36 hours or 36 days, which has direct cost implications on land use and structural waterproofing. In the Price Corridor, where Cisco and Intel have deep basements, the team runs Lugeon testing in fractured caliche to quantify secondary permeability before a drop of concrete is poured.

A caliche layer at ten feet can act like a clay liner, turning your infiltration basin into a pond unless field permeability testing maps the vertical flow barrier.

How we work

At 1,214 feet of elevation and with less than 9 inches of annual rainfall, you would think Chandler’s subsurface is uniformly dry. That assumption has cost local builders millions. The upper 20 feet of soil here is riddled with discontinuous sand stringers and old irrigation channels, creating preferential flow paths that a standard borehole log misses entirely. The grain-size analysis alone cannot tell you how the soil mass behaves hydraulically when subjected to a constant head. That is where the field test earns its keep. Our team runs variable-head Lefranc tests in soil and Lugeon packer tests in fractured or weathered rock, following ASTM D6391 procedures. Data is collected at multiple depth intervals to build a true 3D permeability profile. We cross-check field results with lab permeability on Shelby tube samples, but the field curve is what the civil engineer needs to size pumps, design underdrains, or model mounding beneath a recharge basin. The difference between a 1x10^-4 cm/s and 1x10^-6 cm/s reading is the difference between a gravity system and a mechanical lift station.

Local geotechnical context

The most frequent mistake we see in the East Valley is assuming that a single Perc test at five feet is sufficient for a commercial infiltration system. A contractor will dig a shallow test pit, pour water in, and call it good. Six months later, the retention basin is holding water, mosquitoes are breeding, and the City of Chandler is issuing a notice of violation. The problem is that the critical restrictive layer often sits at 12 or 15 feet, well below a standard backhoe reach. Without a deep Lefranc or Lugeon test, the engineer never sees the low-permeability claystone that will cause water to mound upward into the subgrade of the adjacent building. For stormwater injection wells being pushed by Chandler’s Green Building program, the Lugeon test is non-negotiable. You must know the fracture connectivity and the in-situ stress-dependent permeability of the rock mass. Guessing leads to over-excavation, redesigns, or a permanently wet basement.

Other technical services

01

Lefranc Variable-Head Testing in Alluvial Soils

Installed in uncased boreholes or through hollow-stem augers, this test measures the hydraulic conductivity of specific soil horizons. We run multiple stages to isolate sand lenses from clay layers, giving the project civil engineer a layered model for dewatering or recharge design.

02

Lugeon Packer Testing in Rock and Caliche

Using single or double packer assemblies, we isolate 10-foot intervals in bedrock or heavily cemented caliche. Pressure is stepped up and down to detect fracture dilation or infilling, providing a reliable estimate of secondary permeability for deep excavations and injection wells.

Questions and answers

When does the City of Chandler require a Lugeon test instead of a Lefranc test?

The City typically requires a Lugeon test when the proposed infiltration system or dewatering well extends into cemented caliche or bedrock. The Lugeon method provides fracture permeability data under controlled pressure steps, which is essential for modeling flow through rock discontinuities.

How many test intervals do you recommend for a commercial retention basin?

We recommend a minimum of three test intervals per borehole, spaced to capture the proposed basin bottom elevation, the seasonal high groundwater elevation, and one intermediate point. For basins deeper than 15 feet, we add test intervals at every significant lithologic change.

Can you correlate lab permeability with the field test results?

Yes, we run laboratory permeability tests on undisturbed Shelby tube samples for comparison. However, the field test consistently provides a more representative value because it includes the effects of macro-scale features like root casts, fissures, and sand seams that a small lab specimen cannot capture.
