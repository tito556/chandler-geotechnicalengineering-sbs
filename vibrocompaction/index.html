Chandler sits on the deep alluvial fan of the Salt River, where subsurface conditions often include loose to medium-dense sands extending beyond 30 feet before hitting caliche. These clean to silty sand deposits, typical of the Basin and Range physiographic province, are exactly the kind of profile where vibrocompaction performs well—but only if the design accounts for the transition zones and occasional gravel stringers we see in boreholes across the Price Corridor. Our approach starts with CPT and SPT data to map relative density and fines content across the site, because in Chandler's eastern expansion areas, a uniform grid pattern rarely matches the actual depositional variability. When the fines content stays below 12 percent, we can target a relative density above 70 percent, which meets IBC Table 1806.2 requirements for improved ground under shallow foundations. For sites with higher silt pockets near the Kyrene recharge zone, we often combine the vibrocompaction layout with a pre-treatment CPT program to verify that the soil fabric responds to vibratory energy before mobilizing the full fleet.

In Chandler's desert basin sands, vibrocompaction design that skips post-treatment CPT verification is just expensive guesswork with a vibrator.

Methodology and scope

Chandler sits at roughly 1,200 feet elevation on a gentle slope toward the Gila River Indian Community, and the subsurface here tells a story of Pleistocene flooding cycles. The upper 15 to 25 feet typically classify as SP-SM under ASTM D2487, meaning poorly graded sands with variable silt content—a profile that can densify well but needs careful energy calibration. Vibrocompaction design in this geology means specifying probe spacing, vibration frequency, and hold time based on electric cone tip resistance and friction ratios, not just SPT blow counts. We cross-reference the grain size distribution from wash borings to confirm that the coefficient of uniformity supports particle rearrangement under vibration. A well-designed program in Chandler should achieve a minimum relative density of 70% with probe centers between 6 and 10 feet, depending on the target depth and the presence of the hard caliche layer that sometimes caps the deeper sand unit near the Loop 202 corridor.

Local considerations

A three-story medical office building off Dobson Road started showing differential settlement within eighteen months of construction because the original geotechnical report assumed medium-dense sands at 20 feet based on limited SPT data. The reality was a loose sand lens at 12 to 18 feet that had gone undetected between borings. This scenario replays across the Southeast Valley when vibrocompaction design is treated as a commodity rather than a site-specific engineering task. The risk is not just settlement—it is the potential for differential movement that cracks slab-on-grade floors and misaligns door frames, triggering costly tenant disputes. In Chandler's seismic design category C, loose saturated sands also carry liquefaction potential under the 475-year return period ground motion specified by ASCE 7-22. A properly executed vibrocompaction grid, verified by post-treatment CPT soundings at no less than one test per 2,500 square feet, mitigates both static and seismic settlement risks before structural loads are applied.

Applicable standards

The design methodology adheres to ASTM D1586-18 for Standard Penetration Test procedures, ASTM D2487-17 for soil classification per the Unified Soil Classification System, and ASCE 7-22 for minimum design load criteria.

Associated technical services

01

Site-Specific Vibrocompaction Grid Design

We determine probe spacing, energy input, and treatment depth using CPT tip resistance and friction sleeve data. Grids are designed for uniform densification or targeted treatment of identified loose zones, with hold times calibrated to Chandler's sand gradation.

02

Post-Treatment Verification Testing

CPT soundings at grid centroids and select SPT borings confirm that minimum relative density targets are met. We compare pre- and post-treatment cone resistance profiles and issue a signed verification report for the structural engineer of record.

03

Liquefaction Mitigation Analysis

Using NCEER methodology and site-specific peak ground accelerations from the USGS hazard tool, we estimate post-treatment factor of safety against liquefaction and calculate residual settlement under the design earthquake scenario.

Frequently asked questions

What soil types in Chandler respond well to vibrocompaction?

Clean to slightly silty sands classified as SP or SP-SM under ASTM D2487, with less than 12 to 15 percent passing the #200 sieve, respond best. The alluvial deposits across much of Chandler fall into this range, though pockets of higher silt content near former river channels may require supplemental techniques like stone columns.

How do you verify the ground actually improved after treatment?

We run CPT soundings at grid centroids, typically one test per 2,500 square feet minimum, and compare pre- and post-treatment cone tip resistance and sleeve friction. Spot SPT borings may supplement the CPT data. We require a minimum relative density of 70 percent before signing off.

Does vibrocompaction eliminate liquefaction risk in Chandler?

It reduces risk below the code-required threshold when designed correctly. Using NCEER methodology with site-specific PGA values from the USGS hazard tool, we target a factor of safety above 1.2 against liquefaction triggering. Post-treatment verification confirms that the improved soil mass meets the design intent.
