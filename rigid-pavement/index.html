In Chandler, we’re dealing with a dry, hot climate and subgrades that run from sandy loam to caliche layers you can barely scratch with a pick. When a concrete pavement fails here, it’s rarely the concrete itself—it’s the subgrade support, the joint spacing, or the curling stresses nobody accounted for during design. Our lab team runs flexural strength beams, modulus of subgrade reaction tests, and thermal gradient analyses to lock down a rigid pavement design that won’t crack in July or heave after a monsoon storm. We also tie in the CBR for road subgrade when the project transitions from rigid to flexible sections, so the whole alignment works as one system.

A rigid pavement in Chandler lives or dies by the subgrade support—get the k-value wrong and you’ll see corner cracks within two summers.

Methodology and scope

Chandler’s growth from a small agricultural town to a tech hub means we’re constantly testing on old farm parcels where the soil profile is a mixed bag—irrigation silts, compacted fill, and pockets of expansive clay that move with moisture. A rigid pavement design here has to account for a subgrade that can lose stiffness fast if water gets in. We test for that. Our concrete cylinder breaks and beam flexural tests follow ASTM C39 and ASTM C78, while the k-value determination uses a plate load test right on the prepared subgrade. We also pull cores from trial slabs to verify in-place density and air void distribution before the main pour. For sites near the Price Road Corridor, where truck traffic is heavy, we factor in fatigue loading per the PCA method to size the slab thickness correctly.

Local considerations

We see a clear difference between sites near the Loop 202 Santan Freeway and those farther south toward Hunt Highway. The northern sites sit on older alluvial deposits—dense, well-graded sands and gravels that give you a k-value above 200 pci without much work. The southern parcels can be underlain by silty clays with k-values below 100 pci, and that’s where rigid pavements fail if the designer assumes uniform support. We’ve pulled cores from cracked slabs in industrial parks south of Pecos Road where the subbase had eroded into a void from poor drainage, leaving the concrete edge unsupported. A plate load test on the subgrade and a drainage survey would have caught it. The same goes for alkali-silica reaction—our petrographic exams catch reactive aggregates before they cause map cracking two years in.

Applicable standards

Applicable standards include ASTM C78/C78M-22 for flexural strength of concrete and ASTM D1196/D1196M-21 for nonrepetitive static plate load testing.

Associated technical services

01

Flexural Strength & Mix Verification

We cast and break beams per ASTM C78 to confirm the concrete mix meets the specified modulus of rupture. Trial batches tested at 7 and 28 days, plus maturity method tracking for early-age strength.

02

Subgrade & Subbase k-Value Testing

Plate load tests on the prepared subgrade and cement-treated base to determine the Westergaard modulus of subgrade reaction. We correlate with DCP and CBR values for QA during grading.

03

Joint Layout & Dowel Alignment

Review of contraction joint spacing based on slab thickness, aggregate type, and local temperature swings. We verify dowel bar position with MIT Scan-2 on trial sections before production paving.

Frequently asked questions

What’s the typical slab thickness for rigid pavement in Chandler?

For most commercial and industrial parking lots, we see designs between 6 and 8 inches of plain concrete, depending on the subgrade k-value and the expected truck traffic. Arterial roads and bus lanes often go to 9–11 inches with doweled joints. The AASHTO 1993 method or PCA design software will give you the exact number once we have the k-value and flexural strength data from the lab.

Why does joint spacing matter so much in a hot climate like Chandler?

Concrete expands and contracts with temperature changes, and in the Sonoran Desert the daily swing can exceed 30°F. If joints are spaced too far apart, the slab develops high tensile stresses from curling and thermal movement, leading to random transverse cracking. We typically keep joint spacing at 24 to 30 times the slab thickness and saw-cut within 12 hours of finishing to control where the crack forms.

Do you test the subgrade before the concrete pour?

Yes. We run plate load tests for the modulus of subgrade reaction (k-value) and correlate with dynamic cone penetrometer readings across the site. If the soil is expansive clay or poorly compacted fill, we recommend stabilization or a thicker cement-treated base to provide uniform support and prevent curling cracks.

Can you test existing concrete pavement for distress or remaining life?

We can. We pull cores for compressive strength and petrographic analysis, run falling weight deflectometer or plate load tests to assess the effective k-value and load transfer at joints, and survey cracking patterns. That data feeds into a rehabilitation design—whether it’s dowel bar retrofit, slab replacement, or an asphalt overlay with a stress-absorbing interlayer.
